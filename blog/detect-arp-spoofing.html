How to Detect ARP Spoofing on Your Home Network

Your home router hands out IP addresses. Every device on the network trusts those mappings. ARP spoofing abuses that trust — an attacker on your local network sends fake ARP replies, redirecting your traffic through their machine before it reaches the router. You keep browsing. They read everything.

The scary part: it works silently. There's no error, no slowdown, no warning. Unless you're specifically watching for it, you won't know it's happening.

What Is ARP, and Why Can It Be Spoofed?

ARP (Address Resolution Protocol) maps IP addresses to MAC addresses. When your laptop wants to talk to 192.168.1.1, it broadcasts: "Who has 192.168.1.1? Tell me your MAC." Your router replies. Your laptop caches that reply.

The problem: ARP is stateless. Any device on the network can send an unsolicited ARP reply, and most operating systems will update their cache. No authentication, no verification. This was designed for trusted networks in the 1980s and has never been patched at the protocol level.

Who's Actually at Risk?

If your network is locked down with a strong Wi-Fi password and no guests, your risk is lower — but not zero. ARP spoofing attacks can come from:

  • A compromised device already on your network (malware pivot)
  • A guest device used by someone who's not fully trustworthy
  • An attacker who cracked or brute-forced your Wi-Fi password
  • A rogue device on a shared apartment/building network

Manual Detection: Check the ARP Table

The fastest manual check is to look at your ARP table and flag any IP address that maps to more than one MAC address. Two IPs sharing a MAC means one of them is spoofed.

# Linux / macOS
arp -a

# Windows
arp -a

# Look for duplicate MAC addresses:
# ? (192.168.1.1) at aa:bb:cc:dd:ee:ff [ether] on eth0
# ? (192.168.1.100) at aa:bb:cc:dd:ee:ff [ether] on eth0  ← same MAC = suspicious

If your router's IP and another device's IP share the same MAC, you're likely under an ARP poisoning attack. The attacker's machine is impersonating your gateway.

Using arpwatch for Continuous Monitoring

arpwatch is a classic Unix tool that watches ARP traffic and emails you when a MAC address changes for an IP it's seen before.

sudo apt install arpwatch       # Debian/Ubuntu
sudo systemctl enable arpwatch
sudo systemctl start arpwatch

Configure it to email you by editing /etc/arpwatch.conf. Every time an IP gets remapped to a new MAC, you'll get an alert. It's lightweight and runs as a daemon.

Using Wireshark / tshark for Packet Analysis

If you want to see the attack in real time, filter ARP traffic in Wireshark:

# tshark (CLI)
sudo tshark -i eth0 -f "arp" -Y "arp.duplicate-address-detected"

# Or watch for ARP replies flooding in
sudo tshark -i eth0 -f "arp" | grep "is-at"

Legitimate networks see ARP replies only when a device first joins or when the cache expires. If you see a flood of ARP replies, or the same IP-to-MAC pairing change multiple times per second, that's the fingerprint of an active spoof.

Static ARP Entries (Prevention)

For your most critical devices — the router, a NAS, a work machine — you can set static ARP entries. These can't be overwritten by spoofed ARP replies.

# Linux: add a permanent ARP entry for your router
sudo arp -s 192.168.1.1 aa:bb:cc:dd:ee:ff

# Make it permanent (add to /etc/rc.local or a systemd service):
arp -s 192.168.1.1 aa:bb:cc:dd:ee:ff
Limitation: Static ARP only protects that specific device. It won't detect attacks targeting other devices on the same network. For full coverage, you need network-wide monitoring.

Router-Level Defenses

Some routers support Dynamic ARP Inspection (DAI), which validates ARP packets against a trusted DHCP binding table. Check your router's admin panel under "Security" or "LAN settings." Most consumer routers don't support it — it's typically a feature of business-grade hardware (Ubiquiti, Cisco, Netgear managed switches).
